01 / The original introduction

What do matrices mean?

What do matrices mean to you?

Why do we need a way to represent an array of rows and columns of numbers, and to execute computations and operations between them?

12−20−12=32

In school, you may recall reluctantly performing drill after drills of matrix-vector multiplications mechanically. You may even have been taught to memorize several inane formulas.

abcdxy=ax + bycx + dy

Let's explore a deeper, and a more visual intuition behind what matrices represent.

02 / A vector

Start with a point in space

A vector multiplied by a matrix returns yet another vector — it transforms a vector into a new vector. Let's visualize this transformation with an example.

Let's first think about what the coordinates of a vector represent.

−12

The arrow reaches one unit left and two units up from the origin.

03 / The standard basis

Two directions are enough

In the xy-coordinate plane, any vector can be thought of as the sum of two scaled vectors: the unit vector in the x-direction, and the unit vector in the y-direction.

e₁ =10e₂ =01

These are also known as our standard basis vectors.

04 / Linear combinations

Scale, then add

−12= −110+ 201

The vector's coordinates encode the amount to scale each individual basis vector, before adding them up.

This scaling and addition of vectors is called a linear combination, and every vector can be expressed as a linear combination of basis vectors.

Follow the pink horizontal segment, then the purple vertical segment. Together they reach the cyan vector.

05 / Matrix columns

Move the basis vectors

Did you notice anything similar with the expression for matrix-vector multiplication? A vector multiplied with a matrix can also be expressed as a linear combination; only this time the standard basis vectors are replaced by the columns of the matrix.

12−20−12=32

In other words, a matrix can be viewed as a way of packaging information about the new basis vectors that we want. This is the core insight: a matrix transforms a vector by transforming the original basis vectors.

Again, the transformed vector is a linear combination of the new basis vectors, which are scaled by the coordinates of the original vector.

06 / Transforming space

Every point follows

In that vein, a matrix transformation appears to warp and transform space. To get a visceral feel of this, let's visualize what happens to not just a single vector, but a sample of vectors in space, each multiplied by the same matrix.

In order to make the space less visually cluttered, we can represent each vector with just its tip as a point in space. We'll transform the grid lines along too, overlaying on top a copy of the original.

The transformation appears to rotate and stretch the space, accordingly with where the new basis vectors land.

07 / The connection so far

One rule for the whole plane

Any vector is a linear combination of the basis vectors. The matrix columns tell us where those basis vectors land, and matrix-vector multiplication tells us where every other vector lands.

Under a linear transformation, the origin stays fixed and straight lines stay straight—or collapse to a point.

Now we can ask a new question: when does this transformation keep enough information to be undone?

08 / From vectors to area

Look between the arrows

Watch the same basis vectors move back to the identity matrix. At the end, they form two sides of a unit square, with area 1.

Instead of following just an arrow, follow the whole shaded region. Its four corners transform by the very same rule as every other point.

I =1001

This connects the original picture of transformed space to a new quantity: the determinant.

09 / The determinant

A square becomes a parallelogram

As the basis vectors move, the square stretches and shears into a parallelogram. Its sides are still the matrix columns.

A =2101

For a 2 × 2 matrix, det(A) = ad − bc. Here, 2 × 1 − 1 × 0 = 2.

The transformed square has area |det(A)| = 2. The dashed square stays behind as a reference: its area is still 1.

10 / Keeping information

Nonzero area, a unique way back

The parallelogram has nonzero area. The basis vectors remain independent, so they still span the plane.

Every output corresponds to exactly one input: the transformation is invertible.

Follow the two marked inputs: P = (0.5, 0.5) and Q = (0, 1.5). Their outputs are distinct. We will keep these same two inputs for the next collapse.

This pair illustrates the idea. The nonzero determinant guarantees distinct outputs for every pair of distinct inputs.

11 / Losing a dimension

What if the area becomes zero?

Keep scrolling as the second basis vector moves toward the horizontal axis. The parallelogram becomes thinner, and the two marked outputs move closer.

A →2100

At the end, both columns lie on one line. The square has collapsed to a line segment: area = 0 and det(A) = 0.

P and Q now both produce (1.5, 0). From that output, we cannot know which input we started with. The matrix is not invertible.

12 / Losing both dimensions

The whole plane can collapse

If both columns become zero, the unit square collapses even further—to a single point.

A =0000

Every input lands at the origin. Once again, the determinant and area are zero, and there is no unique way back.

13 / Signed area

Negative does not mean impossible

First, watch the collapsed vectors grow back into the unit square. Then keep scrolling to reflect it across the vertical axis.

A =−1001

The final determinant is −1, but the area is |−1| = 1. A negative determinant means reversed orientation. This reflection is still invertible.

The straight-line animation passes through a singular matrix halfway. That temporary collapse does not make the final reflection singular.

15 / Continuing the original

Beyond two dimensions

So far we've only been talking about matrix transformations in two dimensions on the xy-plane. Do the same intuitions carry over to higher dimensions?

In three dimensions, there is another basis vector in the z-direction. The same determinant idea measures volume instead of area: a zero determinant means the transformed unit cube has zero volume.
